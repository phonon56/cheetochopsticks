What the plan does

Colorado Springs recycles or composts about 16% of its waste today — less than half the national average. The Waste Diversion Action Plan is a 20-year roadmap to reach 37% by 2045 by making recycling and composting easier, cheaper, and clearer for everyone.

Importantly, nothing about your current trash service changes right now; the plan guides improvements over time. Here is what it focuses on and what residents told the City they want.

The five strategies

  • Leverage Colorado’s Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) program
  • Education & outreach
  • Organics management (food scraps & yard waste)
  • Governance & data tracking
  • Construction & specialty waste diversion

What residents asked for

  • 69.6% want curbside yard-waste collection
  • 50.4% want curbside food-waste collection
  • Clearer guidance on what is actually recyclable
  • More drop-off sites and fairer access for apartments & rural areas

How it’s paid for

The study and plan were funded by a grant from Colorado Circular Communities (C3), a state program. Going forward, much of the cost of recycling shifts onto producers under Colorado’s EPR law (HB 22-1355), which began reimbursing recycling services in 2026 — not onto your city tax bill. (The often-quoted “$400 million” figure is the size of El Paso County’s existing recycling economy, not a project budget.)

Transcript

Over two seasons, crews sorted roughly nine thousand pounds of household trash and recycling by hand. The biggest surprise: a large share of what we throw away never needed to be trash at all. Food scraps made up about fifteen percent of the pile, and yard debris another ten percent — material that can be composted instead of buried. The study gives the City a clear, data-backed starting point for the goals in this plan.

The Why

Colorado is the country of the Wild West — wide open land, big sky, and country worth protecting. Keeping it clean and beautiful isn’t nostalgia; it’s the whole point. Every ton we keep out of a landfill is land left for the next generation to ride, hike, and call home.

Land worth keeping

Colorado Springs is on track to rival Denver as Colorado’s largest city by 2050 — already the largest by land area, with population projected to close the gap (Colorado State Demography Office). More people means more waste — unless we change how we handle it. Diverting waste protects open space, water, and the views that drew people here in the first place.

It pays off locally

Recycling and reuse already support more than 2,700 jobs and over $400 million in economic activity across El Paso County (Recycle Colorado / CDPHE economic impact data). Strengthening these systems keeps dollars — and materials — circulating close to home.

Frontier ethic, modern tools

The old code of the West was simple: leave a place better than you found it. WastelessCOS is that same idea with a roadmap attached — clear goals, better access, and honest data so the land stays wild where it should be.

How do I choose a trash hauler?

Colorado Springs has no city-run trash service — private companies compete, and you sign up directly with one. You don’t have to call them all: most haulers have an address checker on their website that shows whether they serve you, your pickup day, and price in a couple of minutes. When comparing, look at four things: (1) does recycling come included or cost extra, (2) cart size and bulk/yard-waste options, (3) whether a bear-resistant cart is required for your area (west of I-25), and (4) contract length and fees. Starting in 2026, the state’s producer-responsibility program is expected to make curbside recycling free through participating haulers.

Frequently asked questions

Why did the City create this plan?

The City lacked comprehensive data and a clear roadmap for waste diversion. A state grant funded a study and this 20-year plan so improvements can be made deliberately as the city grows.

Does my trash or recycling service change now?

No. The plan creates no immediate changes to how trash and recycling are collected. It is a roadmap for improving services over time.

Who pays for expanded recycling?

Under Colorado’s Extended Producer Responsibility law, companies that sell packaging and paper products fund the recycling system. As of 2026, reimbursements to participating haulers have begun, shifting cost away from residents.

Do I have to compost or recycle?

No. Participation is voluntary. The plan focuses on making recycling and composting easier and cheaper, not on mandates.
